Preface

About this book
This book began as a research document on the authority of Jesus Christ over
demons and unclean spirits. It has been developed into a teaching book for prac‐
tical use in deliverance ministry.
   It is written for:
    • pastors and church leaders,
    • deliverance and prayer-ministry teams,
    • Bible school and discipleship teachers,
    • Christians who want a biblical, Christ-centred understanding of spiritual
      authority,
    • anyone seeking a safe, sober, and scripturally grounded approach to
      deliverance praxis.

The book is intentionally Christ-centred before it is demon-centred. It studies
the enemy only enough to understand the victory of the King. It refuses sensa‐
tionalism, fear-driven speculation, and the importing of later demonological sys‐
tems into the biblical text.
   The governing conviction is simple:

      Jesus Christ is not merely more powerful than demons. He is Lord over the entire
      spiritual order.

Safeguarding and scope
This book is a theological and pastoral resource. It is not a substitute for medical
care, psychiatric care, counselling, or emergency services.
   Before any practical use, read Part IV carefully. In summary:
 • Do not physically restrain, hit, choke, or harm anyone.
 • Do not withhold medication, food, sleep, or medical care.
 • Obtain informed consent. A person may stop at any time.
 • Use a two-adult rule. Never minister alone to a minor or vulnerable adult.
 • Refer for psychosis, delusions, hallucinations, self-harm, suicide risk, medical
   symptoms, abuse disclosure, or substance withdrawal.
 • Do not charge for deliverance.
 • Do not diagnose mental illness as demonisation.
 • Do not use recovered-memory techniques or coercive accusation.
 • Keep confidentiality, with clear limits for safety and legal duties.

These safeguards are not a lack of faith. They are part of loving your neighbour.

Introduction
✦


The King at the Centre

T
he ministry of Jesus Christ reveals something extraordinary about the rela‐
       tionship between the Kingdom of God and the spiritual realm: Jesus did
not negotiate with demons, seek permission from them, or depend on their co‐
operation to exercise His authority. He commanded, and they obeyed.
   His authority was not merely a spiritual gift or an extraordinary ability. It pro‐
ceeded from His divine identity, His relationship with the Father, and His rightful
dominion over all creation.
   Consider three foundational distinctions:
    • Authority: The legitimate right to command and exercise jurisdiction.
    • Power: The capacity and might to execute that authority.
    • Dominion: The supremacy of rule over a territory, realm, or domain.

In Christ, these are not competing concepts. His authority is accompanied by the
power to enforce His commands.

The governing thesis of this teaching:

      Jesus Christ is not merely more powerful than demons. He is Lord over the entire
      spiritual order. Demonic spirits operate as created, subordinate beings and have no
      legitimate sovereignty over Him.

This thesis shapes everything that follows. It means that deliverance is not a con‐
test between two equal supernatural forces. It is the confrontation of God’s King‐
dom with an illegitimate rebellion. It means that the minister does not need to be
afraid of the enemy, bargain with the enemy, or become fascinated by the enemy.
The minister points people to the King.
   The recommendation that shaped this book is worth stating at the outset:

    than simply a demonology course. The distinction matters: demonology studies the

That is the aim of this book.

1.4 Why this matters for deliverance
A deliverance ministry that begins with the enemy will become fearful, sensation‐
al, or unbalanced. A deliverance ministry that begins with Christ will become
confident, sober, and pastoral.
    The difference is practical:
    • Fear-based ministry asks, “How strong is the demon?” Christ-centred
      ministry asks, “Who is the King?”
    • Spectacle-based ministry measures success by manifestations. Christ-centred
      ministry measures success by restoration, truth, and discipleship.
    • Technique-based ministry trusts a formula. Christ-centred ministry trusts
      the person and finished work of Jesus Christ.
    • Power-based ministry can become proud. Christ-centred ministry remains
      humble, because authority is received, not owned.

The central Scripture for this study is Luke 4:36 (KJV):

      “And they were all amazed, and spake among themselves, saying, What a word is
      this! for with authority and power he commandeth the unclean spirits, and they
      come out.”

The crowd did not merely marvel at power. They marveled at authority and
power together. Jesus had the right to command, and the ability to enforce the
command.

CHAPTER TWO



The Language of Authority
✦



T
o understand Christ’s authority, we must understand the language Scrip‐
      ture uses to describe authority, power, demons, spirits, and uncleanness.


2.1 Authority — ἐξουσία (Exousia)
G R E E K • STR ON G’S G1849

Meaning: Authority, jurisdiction, right, legitimate power, freedom to exercise
control.
   Exousia concerns the right or authorization to act.
   This distinction is important: someone can possess physical strength without
possessing legitimate authority. Conversely, someone can possess legal authority
and exercise it through the power available to them.


    KEY SCRIPTURES

     • Matthew 28:18 — All authority in heaven and earth has been given to
       Christ.
     • Luke 4:36 — Jesus commands unclean spirits with authority.
     • Luke 9:1 — Jesus gives the twelve disciples power and authority over
       demons.
     • John 17:2 — The Father gives the Son authority over all flesh.
     • Ephesians 1:20–22 — Christ is seated above every principality, power,
       might, and dominion.
    The word exousia appears in Luke 4:36 in the context of Christ’s command over
    an unclean spirit. It is not simply a demonstration of supernatural strength; it is
    the exercise of unquestionable authority.
       Translation note: Matthew 28:18 in the KJV reads, “All power is given unto
    me in heaven and earth.” The Greek word is exousia, “authority.” When quoting
    the KJV, keep “power” and explain the underlying term.

        Nuance: Ephesians 1:20–23 is the fuller range for the exaltation of Christ.
     Either 1:20–22 or 1:20–23 is acceptable, but the book uses 1:20–23 where the
     “all things under His feet” clause is in view.



2.2 Power — δύναμις (Dynamis)
G R E E K • STR ON G’S G1411

Meaning: Might, ability, inherent capacity, miraculous power, effective force.
  Dynamis concerns the ability to accomplish something.
  Consider Luke 9:1:

     “Then he called his twelve disciples together, and gave them power and authority
     over all devils, and to cure diseases.”

Notice the two concepts:
 • Power — dynamis.
 • Authority — exousia.

Jesus did not merely give His disciples an ability to perform extraordinary acts. He
commissioned them with authority to exercise that ability within the scope of His
mission.
   Teaching principle: Authority establishes the right to command; power en‐
ables the execution of that command.
   Nuance from Luke 10:19: The KJV reads, “Behold, I give unto you power to
tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy.” The first
“power” is exousia (authority); the second is dynamis (might). The verse is a strik‐
ing example of the distinction.


2.3 Demon — δαιμόνιον (Daimonion)
G R E E K • STR ON G’S G1140

The New Testament uses daimonion for a demonic or evil spiritual being.
   In the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, this word is
associated with Hebrew terms such as shedim and with false gods or idolatrous
spiritual powers.

   See:
 • Deuteronomy 32:17.
 • Psalm 106:37.
 • 1 Corinthians 10:20.
 • Revelation 9:20.

An important observation: demons are not independent gods. Scripture consist‐
ently places them in opposition to the one true God, without granting them
equality with Him.
   Lexical note: The LXX uses δαιμόνια for the Hebrew shedim in Deutero‐
nomy 32:17 and Psalm 106:37 (LXX 105:37). This supports the source’s connec‐
tion between daimonion and shedim.


2.4 Unclean Spirit — πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον (Pneuma
Akatharton)
G R E E K • S PIR IT + UN CL E AN

Two words:
 • Pneuma — spirit, breath, or wind.
 • Akathartos — unclean, impure, defiled.

The expression refers to a spirit characterized by spiritual impurity.
   We must distinguish this from Levitical ceremonial uncleanness.
   In the Old Testament, a person could become ceremonially unclean through
certain physical conditions without being demonized or morally wicked.
   In the Gospels, however, unclean spirits are associated with demonic opposi‐
tion to God’s holy order.
   The distinction is important: ceremonial impurity is not the same thing as
demonic activity.
   Mark 1:21–28 demonstrates Jesus confronting an unclean spirit in the syn‐
agogue, and the account establishes His unique authority over it.

2.7 The authority/power distinction in pastoral
practice
The distinction is not academic. It changes how we pray and minister.
 • We do not try to generate power by shouting, straining, or performing. Power
   belongs to God.

• We do not claim authority independently of Christ. Authority is delegated by
  the King.
• We do not measure authority by volume or drama. Authority is exercised in
  calm obedience.
• We do not confuse authority with authoritarianism. Christ’s authority is the
  right to command for the good of the person, not to control or harm.
• We do not confuse power with manipulation. The Spirit’s power produces
  truth, holiness, freedom, and love, not coercion.

   3. Never suggest that authority is a personal possession or a spiritual

   4. Remind the team: the authority is His; we are servants.

 • Ephesians 6:12.

Ephesians 6:12 identifies principalities, powers, rulers of darkness, and spiritual
wickedness in high places.
   These descriptions establish the reality of organized spiritual opposition. They
do not, however, give us sufficient information to construct an exhaustive rank-
by-rank demonic government.
   Caution: Much popular teaching maps hierarchies of demons with names,
ranks, and territories beyond what Scripture actually specifies. This book does not
do that. We affirm the reality of organized opposition without pretending to
know more than the text reveals.


3.3 The decisive revelation
Colossians 1:16 declares that all things were created by Christ, including visible
and invisible powers.
  Therefore:
 • Demons are not eternal.
 • Demons are not creators.
 • Demons are not omnipotent.
 • Demons are not omniscient.
 • Demons are not omnipresent.
 • Demons cannot overthrow the sovereignty of God.

These are theological inferences from the createdness of all things, not stan‐
dalone proof texts. They are sound because Scripture presents the powers as cre‐
ated and subordinate.
   Satan’s rebellion does not create a second kingdom equal to God’s Kingdom.
   It creates opposition within God’s creation.

3.4 What this means for deliverance
Because the hierarchy is not a stalemate:
 • We do not need to “balance” God against Satan in our thinking.
 • We do not need to fear that a particular demon might be too strong for Christ.
 • We do not need to bargain, negotiate, or appease.
 • We do not need to map the enemy’s kingdom to feel safe.
 • We can minister from the security of the King’s dominion.

The pastor’s confidence is not “we know all the enemy’s ranks.” It is “we know the
King.”

        Show that the fallen powers are created.
     2. Ask: “Where does popular spiritual-warfare teaching go beyond this
        text?”
     3. Emphasise that the enemy is real but not ultimate.
     4. Use Colossians 1:16 as the anchor for the doctrine of createdness.

CHAPTER FOUR



Seven Encounters with
Demons and Unclean Spirits
✦



J
esus did not teach His authority as an abstract theological concept. He
    demonstrated it repeatedly in human situations.


4.1 Why the encounters matter
The Gospel accounts are not merely case studies in exorcism. They are revelations
of the King. Each encounter shows something about:
    • who Jesus is,
    • what His authority does,
    • how He treats the person,
    • what the Kingdom of God looks like when it arrives.

A deliverance ministry that imitates the Gospels will be Christ-centred, calm,
compassionate, and restorative.


Encounter 1: The synagogue at Capernaum
Scripture: Mark 1:21–28; Luke 4:31–37
   A man in the synagogue had an unclean spirit.
   The spirit recognized Jesus and cried:

       “What have we to do with thee, thou Jesus of Nazareth? art thou come to destroy us? I
       know thee who thou art, the Holy One of God.”

Observe the encounter:
    • The demon recognized Jesus.
    • The demon recognized His identity.


                                                                                         22
DIVINE AUTHORITY




 • The demon understood that Jesus represented its destruction.
 • Jesus did not engage in a prolonged conversation.
 • Jesus rebuked it and commanded it to come out.
 • The spirit obeyed.

Jesus said:

     “Hold thy peace, and come out of him.” — Mark 1:25

The Greek expression translated “Hold thy peace” is Phimōthēti, carrying the
sense of being muzzled or silenced.
   Jesus exercised authority over both the spirit’s presence and its speech.
   Revelation: Demonic recognition of Christ’s identity does not constitute alle‐
giance to Christ. Even demons can recognize divine truth without submitting in
love and obedience.
   Praxis note: Do not mistake a demon’s knowledge of Jesus for saving faith. Do
not be impressed by what the enemy knows. Be impressed by who the King is.


Encounter 2: The man possessed by Legion
Scripture: Mark 5:1–20; Luke 8:26–39; Matthew 8:28–34
   This is one of the most extraordinary accounts of Christ’s dominion.
   The man lived among tombs, was violent, isolated, and uncontrollable by or‐
dinary human restraints.
   When Jesus confronted the spirits, the reply was:

     “My name is Legion: for we are many.” — Mark 5:9

Legion is a Roman military term associated with a large organized military unit. Its
use communicates multiplicity and overwhelming force.
   Yet, observe the reversal:
 • The man could not be restrained by chains.
 • The community could not control him.
 • The spirits recognized Jesus.
 • The spirits pleaded with Jesus.



23
                             Seven Encounters with Demons and Unclean Spirits




 • Jesus determined what would happen.
 • The spirits departed at His command.

The demons requested permission to enter the swine.
  This is a significant detail.
  They did not dictate the terms of the encounter. They made a request.
  Afterward, the man was found:

    “Sitting, and clothed, and in his right mind.” — Mark 5:15

Revelation: Christ’s authority does not merely expel evil. It restores human dig‐
nity, mental soundness, social participation, and freedom.
    This is important because the objective of deliverance is not the spectacle of
demons leaving. It is the restoration of the human person.
    Harmonisation note: Mark and Luke focus on the man who speaks. Matthew
8:28–34 notes that two men met Jesus. The differences are a normal feature of the
Gospel accounts, not a contradiction to be hidden. Teach the unity of the witness
and the pastoral focus of Mark and Luke.
    Praxis note: The goal is not a dramatic deliverance report. The goal is a person
sitting, clothed, in their right mind, restored to community and worship.


Encounter 3: The Syrophoenician woman’s daughter
Scripture: Mark 7:24–30; Matthew 15:21–28
   A Gentile woman approached Jesus because her daughter was grievously
troubled by a demon.
   Jesus responded to her faith, and the daughter was delivered.
   Notice something remarkable: Jesus did not need to be physically present with
the daughter.

6.2 Christ disarmed principalities and powers

This passage is fundamental to understanding spiritual authority.

    • God makes believers alive together with Christ.

The Greek word apekdysamenos in verse 15 is commonly understood in the sense

   It is central to the victory itself.
   Nuance: The participle is debated. Some commentators emphasise “stripping
Himself”; others emphasise “despoiling the powers.” The main pastoral reading is

clear: Christ has disarmed and triumphed over the hostile powers. Avoid building

6.3 The handwriting of ordinances
Colossians 2:14 speaks of the cheirographon tois dogmasin.
 • Cheirographon — a handwritten document or record of obligation.
 • Dogmasin — decrees, regulations, or ordinances.

Paul presents the record standing against us as being cancelled through Christ.
   This passage should not be reduced to an elaborate theory of invisible
courtroom paperwork. Its central message is that Christ’s redemptive work re‐
moves the condemning debt of sin and defeats the hostile powers associated with
humanity’s former bondage.
   Scholarly nuance: The traditional reading is a “certificate of debt.” Kyu Seop
Kim (“The Meaning of χειρόγραφον in Colossians 2:14 Revisited,” Tyndale
Bulletin 68.2 [2017]: 223–239) argues from ancient papyri that cheirographon can
also denote a handwritten declaration or oath concerning religious regulations.
The pastoral point remains: the record against us is cancelled in Christ. Do not
over-systematise the imagery.

8.3 Can demons tempt believers?
Yes.
   Matthew 4:1–11 and 1 Thessalonians 3:5 establish that temptation is real.
   But temptation is not the same as possession.

Distinction:
 • Temptation is an invitation to sin. It is not the same as indwelling.
 • Oppression is external pressure, harassment, or affliction.
 • Influence is a spectrum of suggestion, deception, or accusation.
 • Demonisation is a condition of significant bondage or control.
 • Possession is the strongest and most contested category.

These categories overlap in pastoral experience. They are not always clean. Use
them as tools for discernment, not as labels to slap on people.


8.4 Can demons oppress people?
The Gospels and Acts provide accounts of demonic oppression.
   Acts 10:38 describes Jesus healing those oppressed by the devil.
   Oppression is real. But it is not the same as possession, and it is not the same as
every hardship.


8.5 Can a Christian be demon-possessed?
This question requires careful theological distinction.
   The New Testament clearly teaches that believers belong to Christ and are in‐
dwelt by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19–20; Romans 8:9).
   It also teaches that believers must resist the devil and remain vigilant (James
4:7; 1 Peter 5:8–9).
   Christians differ on whether a genuine believer can experience demonic in‐
dwelling or possession, and the Bible does not provide a direct, universally agreed
statement resolving every aspect of that question.

CHAPTER NINETEEN



Common Questions and
Objections
✦


19.1 “Is deliverance ministry biblical?”

Y
es. Jesus commissioned His disciples to cast out demons (Luke 9:1; Luke
       10:17–20), and the New Testament assumes spiritual conflict (Ephesians
6:10–18; James 4:7; 1 Peter 5:8–9).
    At the same time, deliverance must be Christ-centred, pastoral, and biblically
governed. Not every “deliverance ministry” is healthy.


19.2 “Can a Christian have a demon?”
Christians differ on this question. The New Testament teaches that believers be‐
long to Christ and are indwelt by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19–20; Ro‐
mans 8:9), and it also commands believers to resist the devil (James 4:7; 1 Peter
5:8–9).
   The book does not resolve the debate. It urges careful language, pastoral hu‐
mility, and a focus on Christ’s lordship. Avoid making definitive claims about
every experience.


19.3 “Should we always look for a demon?”
No. Not every problem is demonic. Some problems are medical, psychological, re‐
lational, moral, or simply the result of living in a fallen world. Discernment in‐
cludes ruling out other causes.




                                                                               88
DIVINE AUTHORITY




19.4 “Do we need to know the demon’s name?”
No. Scripture does not command believers to interrogate demons for names,
ranks, or territories. Jesus commanded the spirit to be silent in Mark 1:25. The fo‐
cus should be on Christ’s authority, not on demonic intelligence.


19.5 “Why doesn’t everyone receive immediate
freedom?”
We live in a fallen world, and God’s timing is not ours. Freedom can be gradual.
Some struggles involve trauma, habit, illness, or ongoing discipleship. The team
should not blame the person or promise instant results.


19.6 “Is it wrong to see a doctor or take medication?”
No. Medical care is a gift of God’s common grace. Deliverance ministry must not
replace medical or psychiatric care. Do not tell people to stop medication.


19.7 “What about generational curses?”
Some Christians emphasise generational curses; others do not. The New Testa‐
ment emphasises personal repentance, faith in Christ, and the believer’s new iden‐
tity. If the topic arises, handle it carefully:
 • Do not create fear.
 • Do not charge money to break curses.
 • Do not blame family members.
 • Focus on Christ’s victory and personal discipleship.
 • Refer to pastoral leadership for wisdom.




89
                                               Common Questions and Objections




19.8 “What about deliverance over the internet or by
phone?”
Distance is not a limit to Christ’s authority. But remote ministry has serious limit‐
ations: safeguarding, consent, verification, and follow-up are harder. Use remote
ministry cautiously, with the same safeguards, and prefer in-person care where
possible.


19.9 “What if the person is not a Christian?”
Share the Gospel clearly. Deliverance is not a substitute for conversion. The goal is
reconciliation with God through Christ, not merely relief from symptoms.


19.10 “What if the person is a minor?”
Follow safeguarding policy. Obtain guardian consent. Use a two-adult rule. In‐
volve appropriate professionals. Do not minister alone. Prioritise the child’s safety
and well-being.

20.2 Core ethical commitments
 1. Christ-centredness: The goal is Christ’s glory and the person’s good.
 2. Dignity: Every person is made in God’s image.
 3. Consent: Informed, voluntary, revocable.
 4. Safety: No harm, no restraint, no coercion.
 5. Competence: Know your limits; refer.
 6. Confidentiality: Protect privacy, with clear safety limits.
 7. Accountability: Submit to church leadership and safeguarding policy.
 8. Honesty: No exaggeration, no false promises, no manipulation.
 9. Non-exploitation: No financial gain, no sexual contact, no control.
10. Cultural humility: Respect the person’s background and language.


20.3 Safeguarding policy essentials
    • Two-adult rule.
    • Background checks for team members.
    • Clear reporting procedures.
    • Mandatory reporting compliance.
    • No isolated sessions.
    • No overnight sessions.

20.5 When things go wrong
 • Stop the session.

   Authority (exousia, G1849): The legitimate right to command, exercise juris‐

CONCLUSION



The Supremacy of Christ
✦



T
he entire biblical narrative establishes one central truth:


                            Jesus Christ is Lord.

    He is Lord over creation.
    He is Lord over the visible and invisible realms.
    He is Lord over principalities and powers.
    He is Lord over demons and unclean spirits.
    He is Lord over sin and death.
    And He is Lord over all things.
    Demons may resist, deceive, tempt, accuse, and oppress, but they cannot over‐
throw the sovereignty of God.
    The ministry of Christ reveals that humanity’s freedom is not found in becom‐
ing fascinated with the powers of darkness. It is found in coming under the Lord‐
ship of Jesus Christ.
    The objective of the Gospel is not merely to make people capable of confront‐
ing demons.
    It is to reconcile humanity to God, restore the image of God in human life, es‐
tablish the reign of Christ in the heart, and bring creation under the purposes of
its rightful Lord.
    The ultimate revelation of Divine Authority is not the existence of an enemy
who must be feared. It is the supremacy of a King whose authority cannot be
overthrown.

    “For he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet.” — 1 Corinthians
    15:25 (KJV)
