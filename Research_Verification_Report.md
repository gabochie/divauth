## 1. Purpose and method

This report checks the source document's:

1. Biblical quotations and references.
2. Greek and Hebrew lexical claims, including Strong's numbers.
3. Historical and theological statements that depend on original-language evidence.
4. Cited external sources and links.
5. Claims that require a pastoral-safety caution before use in deliverance ministry.

The method was:

- Direct comparison of quotations against the King James Version (KJV), the translation used by the source.
- Comparison of Greek forms against standard lexical data (Strong's, Thayer, Moulton-Milligan, Abbott-Smith, and standard interlinears).
- Comparison of Hebrew terms against Brown-Driver-Briggs, Strong's, and standard Hebrew lexicons.
- Spot-checking external sources via live web search.
- Reviewing the document's practical claims against recognised safeguarding and mental-health concerns.

## 2. Overall verdict

**The research is substantially accurate.** The central scriptural claims, the Greek terms, the Strong's numbers, and the main lexical definitions check out. The document is also unusually careful in several places: it refuses to make God and Satan equal, it distinguishes ceremonial impurity from demonic activity, it warns against importing later demonological systems into the Bible, and it refuses to attribute all illness to demons.

The needed corrections are mostly matters of **nuance, harmonisation, citation hygiene, and pastoral safety** rather than major factual error.

**Confidence:** High on the biblical and lexical core. Moderate on some theological inferences, which are legitimate interpretations but should be labelled as such rather than presented as lexical facts. Moderate on the external-link apparatus, which contains AI-style tracking parameters and some incomplete inline citations.

## 6. Nuances and corrections to add

These are not fatal errors. They are refinements the teaching book should make explicit.

### 6.1 Matthew 8:28–34 has two demoniacs, not one

Mark 5:1–20 and Luke 8:26–39 describe **one** man. Matthew 8:28–34 describes **two** demoniacs. The source follows Mark/Luke and speaks of "the man." That is legitimate harmonisation, but the book should note the Synoptic difference rather than imply all three accounts are identical.

**Recommended wording:** "Mark and Luke focus on the man who speaks; Matthew notes that two men met Jesus."

### 6.2 The "Legion" detail

"Legion" is correctly identified as a Roman military term. A Roman legion was a large unit, conventionally around 4,000–6,000 soldiers. The text uses it to communicate multiplicity and overwhelming force, not to supply a precise demonic census.

### 6.3 Matthew 28:18: "power" vs "authority"

The KJV says "All power is given unto me," but the Greek word is *exousia*. The source's use of "authority" is correct. When quoting KJV, preserve "power" and explain the Greek term.

### 6.4 Luke 10:19: two different words translated "power"

First "power" = *exousia* (authority to tread). Second "power" = *dynamis* (the enemy's power/might). This actually strengthens the source's authority/power distinction and should be highlighted.

### 6.5 1 John 3:8: "destroy" is a contextual gloss

λύσῃ (*lysē*) literally means "loose," "undo," "dissolve," "break." "Destroy" is a legitimate contextual translation, but the core image is **undoing** the works of the devil, not annihilating persons or denying the reality of evil. The source already says this well.

### 6.6 Colossians 2:15: the participle is debated

ἀπεκδυσάμενος is an aorist middle participle. The source's "stripping or disarming" is standard and contextually sound. Some commentators debate whether the middle voice means "stripping Himself" or "despoiling for Himself." The book should present "disarmed/stripped" as the main reading and note that the exact nuance is debated.

### 6.7 *Cheirographon*: "certificate of indebtedness" is traditional but contested

The traditional reading is a handwritten certificate of debt. Kyu Seop Kim, "The Meaning of χειρόγραφον in Colossians 2:14 Revisited," *Tyndale Bulletin* 68.2 (2017): 223–239, argues from papyri that it can mean a handwritten declaration or oath concerning religious regulations. The source already warns against reducing the passage to "an elaborate theory of invisible courtroom paperwork." That caution is well judged. The book should say "handwritten record/declaration of obligation" and avoid over-systematising the imagery.

### 6.8 "Demons are not omniscient / omnipresent / omnipotent"

These are sound doctrinal inferences from createdness (Colossians 1:16) and from the biblical portrayal of limited spiritual beings. They are not explicit propositional statements in a single verse. The book should label them as **theological inferences**, not as direct lexical claims.

### 6.9 "Christ's authority is not limited by physical proximity"

This is a valid theological inference from the Syrophoenician woman's account (Mark 7:24–30; Matthew 15:21–28). It should be labelled as an inference, not as an explicit statement in the text.

### 6.10 "The Hebrew Bible does not present an elaborate taxonomy of demons"

This is a defensible historical-theological caution. It is not a proof text. It should remain a caution against importing later demonologies into Scripture, not a claim that the Hebrew Bible has no demonology at all.

### 6.11 "Demon-possessed" language

The source uses "demon-possessed" while also noting that Christians differ on whether a believer can be demonised. For pastoral use, prefer the broader language of **demonic oppression, influence, affliction, or bondage**, and reserve "possession" for the strongest cases. Avoid making definitive diagnoses. The New Testament itself uses a range of expressions: *daimonizomai* (to be demonised), having an unclean spirit, being oppressed, being bound by Satan.

### 6.12 Mary Magdalene

Luke 8:2 and Mark 16:9 say seven demons went out of Mary Magdalene. The text does **not** say she was a prostitute. The book should explicitly warn against that traditional but unsupported conflation.

### 6.13 The woman with a spirit of infirmity

Luke 13:11 calls it a "spirit of infirmity," and Luke 13:16 says Satan had bound her. The source correctly refuses to conclude that every illness is demonic. The book should keep that guardrail prominent.

### 6.14 The "sons of Sceva" lesson

The source's reading is sound: the name of Jesus is not a manipulable formula. The text also says the evil spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are ye?" The lesson is about relationship and commission, not about demons keeping a supernatural authorised-minister list.

## 7. Citation and source hygiene

The source document contains several citation artefacts that should be cleaned up before publication:

1. Inline notes such as "only.bible +1," "StudyLight.org +1," and "Saint Louis +1" are incomplete and cannot be verified as formal citations.
2. External links include tracking parameters such as `?utm_source=chatgpt.com`. These should be stripped.
3. The external resources are real and useful, but they are not all primary sources. For a teaching book, prefer:
   - Standard critical texts (NA28/UBS5 for the Greek New Testament; BHS for the Hebrew Bible).
   - Standard lexicons (BDAG, Thayer, LSJ, HALOT, BDB).
   - Reputable commentaries.
   - Peer-reviewed articles where specific claims are contested.
4. The "Further academic and biblical study resources" list is legitimate as a starting point. The MDPI article "Exousia and Conflict in the Gospel of Mark" by Judith König, *Religions* 14.10 (2023): 1318, was verified as a real, open-access article. The LARES lexicon entry for *daimonion* was fetched and verified as a real scholarly resource; it also confirms the Septuagint use of *daimonion* for Hebrew *shedim*, *elilim*, and *sairim* (Deuteronomy 32:17; Psalm 96:5; Isaiah 13:21). The Oxford Bibliographies exorcism entry and the "Jesus and Non-Levitical Impurity" dissertation link returned HTTP 403 during verification, which is consistent with subscription or access restrictions; they are plausible but were not independently confirmed. Do not present them as verified.
5. The Strong's dictionary PDF link is a secondary, older reference. For serious study, use BDAG and the critical editions directly.

**Recommendation:** Keep the resource list, remove tracking parameters, replace incomplete inline citations with full references, and add a short "Sources and translations" note in the book.

## 8. Theological claims that are interpretations, not lexical facts

The following claims in the source are theologically defensible but should be presented as **interpretation** rather than as direct word-study conclusions:

- "Jesus is not merely more powerful than demons; He is Lord over the entire spiritual order."
- "Satan's rebellion does not create a second kingdom equal to God's Kingdom. It creates opposition within God's creation."
- "Demons are not eternal, creators, omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent."
- "Christ's authority is not confined by geographical distance."
- "The objective of deliverance is the restoration of the human person, not the spectacle."
- "Spiritual authority must never become a source of personal pride."
- "The believer's greatest security is not a particular deliverance technique but being established in Christ."

These are sound, Christ-centred conclusions. The book should mark them as **teaching** or **doctrinal inference**, not as claims that the original languages prove by themselves.

## 9. Pastoral-safety review

The source is already cautious in important ways:

- It distinguishes temptation, oppression, influence, psychological distress, and possession.
- It says deliverance ministry should not replace medical or mental-health care.
- It refuses to attribute every illness to demons.
- It warns against treating the name of Jesus as a magical formula.
- It warns against pride in spiritual authority.
- It emphasises restoration and discipleship, not spectacle.

The teaching book strengthens these safeguards with explicit protocols:

1. **No physical restraint, hitting, choking, or withholding medication.**
2. **No sleep or food deprivation.**
3. **Informed consent and the right to stop at any time.**
4. **Two-adult rule and safeguarding for minors and vulnerable adults.**
5. **Referral for psychosis, self-harm, suicide risk, medical symptoms, abuse disclosure, and substance withdrawal.**
6. **No charging for deliverance.**
7. **No secret sessions, no isolation from family or medical care.**
8. **No diagnosing mental illness as demonisation.**
9. **No recovered-memory techniques or coercive accusations.**
10. **Confidentiality with clear limits for safety and legal duties.**

These safeguards are not a dilution of the teaching. They are part of faithful pastoral practice.
